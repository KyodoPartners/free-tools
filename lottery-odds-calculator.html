The honest lottery math

Real odds, what a ticket is actually worth, and a simple demonstration of something the whole industry would rather you didn't test: no amount of statistics picks better numbers than pure chance.

Everything here is plain math you can check. There are no predictions and no "systems" — because for a fair, independently drawn lottery, no way of choosing numbers makes any number more likely to come up.

♦ Play for fun, within a budget you'd be fine losing. If the fun stops, call the National Problem Gambling Helpline: 1-800-522-4700 (24/7, free, confidential).

The "best system" vs. a real draw

Here's the experiment people run when they're sure numbers can be beaten: take every statistical method — most-frequent, hot, cold, pairs, balanced — mash them together into one "best" combined line, then check it against what actually came up. Run it across many past draws and compare it to a plain random Quick Pick. Watch what happens.

What a ticket is actually worth

The "expected value" of a ticket is what you'd win on average per ticket if you played forever. It folds in the real odds, the cash-vs-annuity choice, taxes, and the chance you'd have to split the jackpot with other winners.

—
Enter the jackpot and press calculate.

This uses the jackpot only; small fixed prizes add a few cents and don't change the conclusion. A negative number means the ticket is worth less than it costs — which, for essentially every big lottery, it is.

The "numbers above 31" effect

Choosing numbers above 31 does not improve your odds — nothing does. But most people play birthdays, so their numbers cluster in 1–31. If you win on a line that includes higher numbers, you're less likely to be sharing the prize. Same chance of winning; a bigger slice if you do.

The birthday share is an estimate you can adjust — real figures vary by game. The win probability is identical for both lines; only the expected split changes.

Hot & cold numbers: a simulator

"Hot" numbers come up a lot; "cold" ones are "overdue." This runs real random draws in your browser, finds the hot and cold numbers from that history, then tests whether betting on them beats a random line on the next draws. Because each draw is independent, it never does.

So what's the takeaway?

Every tool on this page agrees on one thing: a lottery ticket is entertainment with a known, negative expected value, and no method of picking numbers changes your odds. If you play, play for the fun of it, with money you can afford to lose.

The interesting part isn't beating luck — it's seeing clearly what's luck and what isn't. Deciding where to put money when the odds can be studied is a different skill. That's what the rest of our free business calculators are for.
